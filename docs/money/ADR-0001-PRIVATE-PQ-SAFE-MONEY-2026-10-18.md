## 1. The decision

### 1.0 The question before the decision

What can an Igneum user do more safely, more cheaply or more easily than with the best existing alternative, and why does achieving it require this protocol rather than a wallet on an existing chain? Answered candidate by candidate, each against the best alternative for it; the verdict first.

**Verdict: one candidate survives, on paper and undemonstrated; three do not.** The one that survives is a protected balance with chosen recovery inside a shielded pool, authorised PQ, on mining a chip cannot take. Nothing is demonstrated, so no advantage is claimed; the record below says why each of the other three is a wallet's job or someone else's chain, and why the fourth needs this chain.

1. **Private payments, against Monero and Zcash.** Not more safely, cheaply or easily. As designed, a shielded transfer here is an estimated 85 to 145 KB against Monero's about 2 KB and Zcash's 9,160 bytes (section 4.5), it is slower to prove, and a new pool's anonymity set is a fraction of theirs. The one difference, hash-based linkage and spend authority that a QC adversary cannot break, is one Zcash has announced it is building (a PQ target within 12 to 18 months of May 2026, section 4.2) and Monero's researchers are studying. Payment privacy alone does not justify this chain; a user who wants only that is better served today by Monero or Zcash, and the record says so.
2. **A protected balance and chosen recovery, against a Safe-style smart-account vault on Ethereum.** Not more safely, cheaply or easily in public. A Safe with a recovery module already gives guardians, a threshold, a delay and an owner's cancellation (Candide's documentation), and modules can add limits and timelocks; it runs on a chain with far more tooling and a deeper audit history than this one. The vault's rules on their own are a wallet-and-contract job on an existing chain and do not justify a chain. What a Safe cannot give is privacy (every balance, delay and recovery is public) or PQ authorisation (secp256k1), and that is the fourth candidate, not this one.
3. **PQ ownership, against Quantus.** Not on its own. Quantus gives ML-DSA accounts, a hash-based wormhole and user-set reversibility today (section 4.2). A user who wants only PQ ownership is served there now. The difference this chain offers is in the security foundation (three GPU lanes read against the chip case, against a proof of work that a chip builds directly), which matters to the network's integrity, not to what the user does day to day; it is not a user-facing advantage on its own.
4. **A protected balance and chosen recovery inside a shielded pool, PQ, on chip-resistant mining.** This is the candidate tested hardest, and the one that survives on paper. No existing system gives it: an Ethereum vault is public; Monero and Zcash have no programmable spend policy inside their shielded protocols and could add one only by a protocol change; Quantus's wormhole hides a link, not an amount, and has no policy inside it; a programmable privacy chain (Aztec, APPROXIMATE from memory: private contracts proven client-side on a curve-based system, no proof of work) could express a private vault but not a PQ one. Why it needs the protocol and not a wallet: a delay, a cancellation key and an aggregate limit protect against a thief who holds the everyday key, and such a thief uses any wallet they like; the rule must therefore be enforced by consensus, inside the spend statement of the pool, where no wallet can opt out of it. On a public chain that place is a contract; inside a shielded pool it is the circuit, and no existing pool's circuit carries a policy. That is the single reason this design is a chain and not a wallet. It survives only on paper: section 4.6 says what must be measured before it is more than that, and section 9 answers the same question against the comparison table.

What this means for the programme: three of the four features (private payments, a public vault, PQ accounts) exist elsewhere or belong in a wallet; the record has found one feature that needs this chain, and the whole of Money is judged on whether that one can be built and measured. If stage 1 of section 6 fails its thresholds, the sentence at the top of this page becomes "no demonstrated advantage; Money pauses as research" and the rest of the page stays as the record of why.

### 1.1 The decision

Igneum's purpose is private digital money: a currency, IGN, that an ordinary person can hold privately, protect from the loss of an everyday key, recover under rules they chose, and verify with a small computer, secured by a proof-of-work network of ordinary graphics cards on three lanes that no special chip can take over. The privacy is a shielded pool on the existing chain: notes committed by a hash, spent by proving knowledge of a secret in a hash-based (STARK-family) proof, nullified so no note spends twice, with amounts, senders and recipients hidden from the ledger and from the network's miners. The PQ requirement is architectural: every secret that authorises a spend or a recovery is protected by a hash-based or lattice-based primitive and never by an elliptic curve, so no QC forges a spend or a recovery by solving a discrete logarithm; what remains is the hash and lattice assumptions listed in section 4.3, and nothing is claimed beyond them. That property is claimed in public only when the complete authorisation path, keys, proof system, note encryption and vault roles, has passed review as one construction. Nothing on the chain is sold to anyone outside it: no paid compute, no proving marketplace, no job submitters. The chain's own proof machinery exists only because privacy needs it, and because anyone should be able to verify the rules on a phone.

The holder proposition, in the holder's words:

- Your money stays private. Who paid whom, and how much, is not on the ledger.
- Your everyday key cannot empty your savings. Savings sit behind a delay and a second key that can stop a withdrawal but cannot redirect it.
- Recovery follows rules you choose. You name who can replace your keys, how long it takes, and what they can never do; the everyday key cannot change those rules.
- Ownership is PQ by design. That sentence is said in public only when the whole path, every key that can spend or recover, has passed review.

## 2. The two experiences

One currency, IGN. The first release carries native IGN transfers only. Public contracts remain what they are today, EVM accounts at 0x addresses with public balances; the wallet labels every move between the shielded pool and a public account as a privacy boundary, in those words, before the person confirms it.

### 2.1 Spend

- Private everyday payments: a payment is a shielded transfer. The ledger records two nullifiers, two note commitments, two ciphertexts and one proof; it records no sender, no recipient and no amount.
- Receiving: a shielded address is a commitment to a spend key and an encapsulation key. Receiving needs no action by the recipient; the wallet finds its notes by scanning the chain's outputs with its own keys, on the person's own machine or node.
- The owner chooses what the protocol exposes: a receipt for one payment, and nothing else from the ledger. A recipient can always disclose what they received, and anyone given a viewing credential can keep or share it. The payer or the payee can produce, for one transaction, a receipt that reveals exactly that transaction's amount, recipient address and memo, and proves them against the ledger's commitment, revealing nothing about any other payment, balance or address. The receipt names what it reveals in words before it is produced (section 8).

### 2.2 Vault

Protected savings, on paper until the payment construction is settled (section 6; the stop condition in section 4.6). The rules the vault must keep, whatever its construction:

- Delayed withdrawals: a withdrawal from the vault is announced, waits a delay the owner chose, then completes; during the delay it is visibly pending in the wallet.
- A separate cancellation key: a second key, held apart from the everyday key, can cancel a pending withdrawal. It can cancel; it cannot redirect, spend or change the policy.
- A user-chosen recovery authority: the owner names an authority (their own cold key, a set of people, a hardware device, or none) that can replace the vault's keys through an explicit process with its own, longer delay, announced on the ledger, cancellable by the cancellation key during that delay.
- The everyday key can never shorten a delay, raise a limit, or replace the recovery authority.
- Repeated small withdrawals cannot bypass the aggregate limit: the vault keeps a running total per period inside its own state, and a withdrawal that would carry the total over the limit is refused, whatever its individual size.
- Policy changes (delays, limits, the recovery authority) need the recovery authority, never the everyday key alone (the form sections 7 and 9 already assume), and take their own delay before they apply; what a vault with no recovery authority set may change, and under which key, is a stage 5 design item and is not decided here.
- Pending funds are visibly pending: the wallet shows what is available, what is protected, what is leaving and when, and what is being recovered and by whom.

## 3. The five security requirements

| requirement | what it means | what would fail it |
|---|---|---|
| Payment privacy | The ledger, the miners and a wallet server learn neither sender, recipient nor amount of a shielded transfer, and cannot link a spend to the note it spends. | A linkable nullifier; an amount readable from the ledger; a network or wallet-server leak that ties an address to an IP or a query pattern; a small anonymity set presented as a large one. |
| Supply integrity | No shielded transfer creates value. The pool's total value never exceeds what verifiably entered it, and anyone can check that number from public data. | A proof-system soundness bug; a hash collision in the commitment or nullifier scheme; a missing turnstile so a counterfeit inside the pool can leave it unnoticed. |
| PQ-resistant ownership | Every authorisation that can move funds or replace keys, the everyday spend, the cancellation, the recovery and the policy change, rests only on hash-based or lattice-based assumptions. | Any curve-based signature or proof anywhere on the spend or recovery path; a public-side key (secp256k1) that can act on vault funds; a claim made before the whole path is reviewed. |
| Theft containment | The loss of the everyday key loses at most the everyday balance plus one period's limit; the loss of the cancellation key alone loses nothing; the loss of the recovery authority alone loses nothing within its delay. | A path by which one key both spends and changes policy; a delay the everyday key can shorten; a withdrawal split that evades the aggregate limit; a recovery that completes without a visible, cancellable delay. |
| Independent verification within the minimum node's measured budget | A node on the minimum supported machine (4 cores of the Intel N100 class, 8 GB, a 128 GB SSD, a 50 Mbit/s link; docs/reliability/DEVNET.md) validates every block, shielded proofs included, inside the whole-block budget of 250 ms at p99, and its disk and bandwidth hold at the stated transaction rate. | A proof the minimum node cannot verify in time; chain growth that fills the disk inside a year at the stated rate; a sync that needs a trusted checkpoint or a server's word. |

Networking and wallet-query privacy are requirements in their own right, named here so they are not forgotten: the IP address a transaction is sent from, the IP a wallet syncs from, and the pattern of a wallet's queries to a server can each link a person to a payment that the ledger itself hides. The design's answer is in section 4.5 (scanning without a server) and section 7 (the malicious wallet-server campaign); the launch sentence makes no privacy claim that the network layer has not been measured against.

## 4. The hardest question: privacy, PQ-resistant ownership and practical node cost in one construction

### 4.1 What the chain is today

The chain is an EVM-style chain: accounts at 20-byte 0x addresses, balances in wei, transactions signed with secp256k1 ECDSA under the EIP-155 and EIP-1559 signers (node/core/types/transaction_signing.go), the EIP-1559 fee market with a 1 gwei floor, the base fee burned and the tip to the sealer (docs/economics/FEE-RULE-2026-10-15.md). Blocks come every 12 seconds; the header carries the proof-of-work fields and the mining-share fields (the bond key, the shares root, the coordinate, the ticket; node/core/types/block.go). Mining rewards settle through 64-block payout lists: the coinbase of a block with a list is the list root's 20-byte prefix, an address nobody holds, and the subsidy plus tips are paid pro rata to the list's keys at the window's close, exact to the wei and reconciled by a public ledger (docs/settlement/LEDGER.md). Supply is 1,000,000,000 IGN by the decision of 18 October 2026 (docs/tokenomics/SUPPLY-2026-10-18.md, on main), the shape of the schedule unchanged. The wallet is a three-process desktop application (window, engine, signer) with 24-word BIP-39 backups, secp256k1 keys on the Ethereum path, watch-only mode and a rule that nothing on screen says "safe" or "final" (docs/product-b/DESIGN.md). None of that signing is PQ, and none of it is private; the shielded pool is added beside it, and the public side stays public.

### 4.2 The candidates

**(a) A shielded pool with hash-based commitments and nullifiers, proven by a hash-based STARK-family proof, with the spend authority inside the proof.** A note is (value, recipient key commitment, randomness); its commitment is a hash of the three; the commitments sit in an append-only Merkle tree; a spend proves, in zero knowledge, that a commitment is in the tree, that the prover knows the spend secret behind the recipient key commitment, that the nullifier is the hash of the note and that secret, and that inputs equal outputs plus the fee. The proof system commits to polynomials with Merkle trees of hashes (FRI), so its soundness rests on the hash alone; succinct arguments of this family are proven secure in the QROM (Chiesa, Manohar and Spooner, ePrint 2019/834). The spend authority is knowledge of a hash preimage, bound to the transaction's bytes through the proof's public inputs, so no signature object is needed on the shielded side; a lattice signature (ML-DSA) is used beside the proof only where a key must act outside a proof (the public side; later the vault's roles when they are public accounts). Note encryption to the recipient uses a lattice KEM (ML-KEM). Every piece is standardised or deployed somewhere; the combination, as one circuit with measured sizes, is not.

**(b) Zcash Orchard-style curve-based proving.** Halo 2 over the Pasta curves, note commitments and nullifiers on the curve, RedPallas spend authorisation, ECDH note encryption. Small and fast today: a Halo 2 proof is 4,992 bytes for one action and 2,272 bytes per further action (zebra, orchard shielded_data), and a two-action fully shielded transaction is 9,160 bytes (zcash/zcash issue 4946). Not PQ: against an adversary with a cryptographically relevant QC, the discrete logarithm on Pasta falls, and with it, in order of consequence: (1) confidentiality of every recorded ciphertext, because the ephemeral secret behind each output's key agreement can be recovered from public data, so amounts, memos and recipients of the whole history are readable, retroactively, by anyone who stored the chain ("harvest now, decrypt later"); (2) address unlinkability, because the incoming viewing key behind a known address is recoverable; (3) proof soundness and spend authorisation, so counterfeits and forged spends become possible from the day the machine exists, bounded by the pool's turnstile. The Zcash project's own statements put the most immediate QC risk on privacy rather than theft and name a programme (Tachyon, "QC recoverability") to remove in-band secret distribution and migrate funds (Bitfinex education page; Decrypt, May 2026). The "when": (1) and (2) apply to data already on the ledger the day a machine exists; (3) applies to new transactions from that day.

**(c) Monero-style ring signatures and confidential amounts.** CLSAG ring signatures with ring size 16 (docs.getmonero.org technical specs), Pedersen commitments with Bulletproofs range proofs, stealth addresses by ECDH, a typical two-input two-output transaction of about 1.9 to 2.5 kB under CLSAG (getmonero.org, the CLSAG audit post), and FCMP++ (full-chain membership proofs) on the way with proofs estimated at 3 to 5 KB per input and 10 to 20 ms to verify (arnoldnakamura FCMP++ explainer, APPROXIMATE). Which parts are PQ: the hiding of a Pedersen commitment is information-theoretic, so the commitment alone never reveals an amount to any adversary; and RandomX is a proof of work, not a cryptographic assumption. Which parts are not: the amount is also sent to the recipient under an ECDH-derived mask, so a QC adversary reads every amount from that channel; stealth addresses fall with ECDH, so every output links to its address; the key image is a function of the one-time key's secret, which the adversary recovers from the public one-time key, so the true spender in every ring is identified and the history is traceable; CLSAG and the binding of Pedersen commitments fall with the discrete logarithm, so forgery and counterfeiting become possible. As of mid-2026 Monero has no deployed PQ component (arXiv 2512.13333, APPROXIMATE as a survey's reading).

**(d) Quantus, as a benchmark.** A Substrate chain with ML-DSA (CRYSTALS-Dilithium) signatures on transparent accounts, Poseidon2 as the hash, a Plonky2 proof layer, and a "zk-wormhole" modelled on EIP-7503: funds are burned to an unspendable address derived from a secret, and a proof of knowledge of that secret later mints them to a fresh account, breaking the link between the two (Immunefi audit scope; EIP-7503 on ethereum-magicians). ML-DSA-87 is the highest setting (public key 2,592 bytes, signature 4,627 bytes; Go crypto/mldsa), the wallet's default ML-DSA-65 (signature 3,309 bytes); a transparent transfer is about 7.3 KB and the chain targets 12-second blocks with a 100-block pruning rule (iq.wiki's reading of the whitepaper, APPROXIMATE). What it gives: PQ authorisation on every transparent account from day one; a break-the-link transfer whose proof is hash-based (FRI over Goldilocks with Poseidon2); user-controlled transaction reversibility. What it does not give: amount privacy (a burn's amount is public, and the anonymity set of a wormhole exit is the set of burns it could be, with the cost of plausible deniability discussed on ethresear.ch), and a chip-resistant proof of work: its QPoW is one Poseidon2 sponge over Goldilocks with a double squeeze and no memory term, a fixed-function field-arithmetic hash that a chip builds directly (the audit of record docs/resistance/QUANTUS-CHIP-AUDIT-2026-10-18.md: a chip at about 47x the 4090's hashes a joule by the model, the band 23x to 62x, MODELLED, none built).

### 4.3 The chosen candidate: (a), the exact construction

Notation: H is the in-circuit hash (section 4.7 names the choice and its risk); B is BLAKE3 for everything outside the circuit, the chain's own hash family.

1. **Keys, from one 32-byte seed s.** sk_spend = H(s, "spend"); nk = H(s, "nullifier"); the viewing secret ivk = H(s, "view"); the ML-KEM-768 key pair (ek, dk) generated from H(s, "kem") as the seed. The recipient key commitment is pk = H(sk_spend, nk). An address is (pk, ek, d), 32 + 1,184 + 11 bytes, about 1.2 KB: long, carried as a QR code or a link, never typed. ML-KEM-768 encapsulation keys are 1,184 bytes and ciphertexts 1,088 bytes (FIPS 203, as the Go crypto/mlkem documentation lists them). An optional short form, a 32-byte B-hash of the address with the full address published once on the chain, is allowed and labelled as a public registration.
2. **Notes and commitments.** A note is (v, pk, r) with v a 128-bit amount in wei carried as four 32-bit limbs, each range-checked in the circuit (the stage-one join's value rule, docs/money/STAGE1-JOIN-2026-10-18.md section 4: 64 bits capped a note at 18.4 IGN against a supply of 10^27 wei), and r 256 bits of randomness; cm = H(H(v, pk), r), the join's nested two-to-one form. Commitments go into an append-only Merkle tree of depth 32 over H; the root is the anchor.
3. **Nullifiers.** nf = H(nk, cm, position). Published with the spend; a block that contains a published nullifier twice, or one already in the nullifier set, is invalid.
4. **The spend statement (one proof per transaction, two inputs, two outputs, the common case).** Public inputs: the anchor, nf_1, nf_2, cm_out1, cm_out2, the public value balance v_pub (positive when unshielding, negative when shielding, zero for a private transfer), the fee, and the public output address. The stage-one join binds these as one statement hash, H folded over the domain word (the chain id and the statement kind) and the public inputs, in place of a tx_hash over the carrier's bytes; the output ciphertexts are therefore not bound by the statement in the join's form, and binding B(ciphertexts) into the statement hash is the one-word change named for the vault cut (the join's section 9). Witness: the two input notes, their Merkle paths, sk_spend and nk for each, the two output notes. The circuit checks: each input's cm is at its path under the anchor; pk = H(sk_spend, nk) for each input; nf_i = H(nk, cm_i, position_i); cm_out_j = H(v_j, pk_j, r_j); v_1 + v_2 = v_out1 + v_out2 + fee + v_pub with every value in range; and that the statement hash is the public input it was proven against (so the proof cannot be moved to another record, another kind or another chain). The spend authority is the knowledge of sk_spend; there is no separate signature. A fully shielded transaction therefore carries no public signer: it is a new transaction type the node accepts with no ECDSA signature, whose fee is the statement's public fee paid from the pool's value (the base fee burned, the tip to the sealer, under the fee rule of section 4.1), and whose relay and mempool rules are a stage 1 design item.
5. **The proof.** A STARK over a small field with FRI, H-friendly, with recursion available but not required at the first stage; the Fiat-Shamir hash is B. Target: 128 bits of security under the proof system's PROVEN soundness bound, or, where the deployable parameters rest on a conjecture, the conjecture named on the parameter page (section 4.7).
6. **Note encryption.** For each output: (ct_kem, K) = ML-KEM-768.Encaps(ek_recipient); the note plaintext (v, r, d, memo up to 128 bytes) is encrypted under K with an AEAD; the sender also stores an outgoing record under a key derived from its own seed so it can later produce a disclosure receipt. No ephemeral curve point anywhere.
7. **The turnstile.** The node keeps one public number per pool: value shielded in (and, after section 5, minted in) minus value unshielded out and fees paid out of the pool; a block that would take it negative is invalid (Zcash's ZIP 209 rule, cited for the form). It bounds the damage of a soundness failure to what entered the pool and makes a counterfeit visible the moment it tries to leave.
8. **The disclosure receipt.** For one transaction the payer or the payee reveals (v, pk_recipient, r, memo) for one output together with the note's position; a verifier recomputes cm and checks it in the tree. The receipt proves exactly that output and nothing else; it is not a viewing key. It does not limit who can disclose: a recipient can always disclose what they received, and anyone given a viewing credential can keep or share it.
9. **The public side.** Shielding is a public transaction from a 0x account into the pool (a public amount, a public sender, a new commitment); unshielding is a spend with v_pub positive paying a 0x account. Both are privacy boundaries and the wallet says so.

Security assumptions, in full: preimage, second-preimage and collision resistance of H and of B (hash security of 128 bits against a QC adversary from 256-bit outputs, Grover's square root allowed for); knowledge soundness and zero knowledge of the STARK in the QROM; the FRI proximity bound at the chosen parameters; the IND-CCA security of ML-KEM-768 (module LWE); nothing on a curve.

### 4.4 What stays protected against a QC adversary, and what does not

| property | rests on | against a QC adversary |
|---|---|---|
| Forgery of a spend (spending a note one does not own) | preimage resistance of H; STARK knowledge soundness | PROTECTED. No curve anywhere on the path. |
| Counterfeiting (creating value inside the pool) | STARK soundness; collision resistance of H; the turnstile | PROTECTED, and bounded by the turnstile even under a soundness failure. |
| Double spend | nullifier uniqueness under H | PROTECTED. |
| Linking a spend to the note it spends | the nullifier is a hash of a secret | PROTECTED. The ledger never gives a QC adversary a curve point to invert. |
| Hiding of the amount in the commitment | H with 256-bit randomness | PROTECTED (computational, 128 bits post-Grover). |
| Confidentiality of the recorded ciphertext (amount, memo, recipient tag) against harvest now, decrypt later | ML-KEM-768 (module LWE) | PROTECTED under the standard's assumption. If module LWE falls, every recorded ciphertext is readable retroactively: amounts, memos and which address received each note. Spending remains impossible for the adversary, because the spend secret is never in a ciphertext. This is the one assumption outside the hash family and it is stated as such. |
| Address unlinkability from the address itself | the address is a hash commitment plus a KEM key | OPEN. As written in section 4.3, two addresses (pk, ek, d) under one seed carry the same pk and ek in the clear and are linkable by inspection; the diversifier d does nothing yet. A per-d derivation (pk_d from pk and d inside the circuit, a KEM key pair per d, the wallet scanning against every d it has issued) is the stage 1 design item, and no unlinkability between one holder's addresses is claimed until it is settled and measured. |
| The public side (0x accounts, shielding and unshielding amounts) | secp256k1 today | NOT PROTECTED and NOT PRIVATE. Funds held publicly are forgeable by a QC adversary and are visible to everyone today. The migration of the public side to ML-DSA is a separate decision, not this page's. |
| Network metadata (IP, timing) | nothing cryptographic | NOT PROTECTED by this construction; see sections 3 and 7. |

The sentence this table licenses, once reviewed and under the assumptions of section 4.3: a QC adversary cannot take funds from the pool or forge a recovery; the worst it can do, under a lattice break, is read the amounts and recipients of recorded payments.

Key backup: the 32-byte seed, shown as the wallet's 24 words, restores everything: spend, nullifier, viewing and KEM keys, every diversified address, and the outgoing records needed for disclosure receipts. There is no second secret to lose.

Scanning: the wallet downloads every output's ciphertext from its own node or from any node, tries each with its own decapsulation key, and keeps the notes that open. No server learns which outputs opened, because every output is downloaded. A wallet that cannot afford the download may hand a viewing key to a server; the wallet then says, in words, that the server sees every amount and payment of that wallet, and the launch sentence's privacy claim does not cover that mode.

Restore from the words: a rescan from the wallet's birthday height; the nullifier set tells it which of its notes are spent, so a restore from an old backup shows the true balance, never a stale one (section 7 tests it).

### 4.6 The stop condition, applied

The rule: if the combined design needs an unsubstantiated construction, or exceeds the minimum node's budget, the page says "Money remains research" and what would have to change, and the claim is not weakened.

The reading. Every primitive is substantiated on its own: hash-based commitment and nullifier schemes are the form Zcash has run since Sapling; FRI-based STARKs with Poseidon-class hashes are in production in several systems and have a QROM security proof; ML-KEM and ML-DSA are NIST standards with fixed sizes; spend authority as knowledge of a hash preimage inside the proof is the form ZK-ACE measured. The COMBINATION, one circuit with this statement, this hash and these parameters, has no published implementation and no measured proof size, verification time on an N100-class machine, or phone proving time; every such figure above is an estimate. The budget reading on the estimates: verification fits the 250 ms block budget at a few dozen transfers a block; disk fits at 1,000 transfers a day with proofs kept, and at 10,000 a day only if proof bytes are pruned after finality and a syncing node re-verifies the history it downloads (at 50 Mbit/s, a year of 10,000-a-day history is 310 to 530 GB, 14 to 24 hours of download, and, at 10 ms a proof on one core, about 10 hours of verification, both within reach but not yet measured); per-block recursive aggregation would cut the sync cost further but adds a prover duty to the sealer and is not needed for the first stage.

**Verdict: Money remains research.** Not because a blocker was found, but because the construction's whole has no measurement yet and the hash choice carries an open cryptanalytic question (section 4.7). (The panel's third reading, 19 October 2026: rows 1, 3, 4 and 5 below are measured and PASS on the lab chains (docs/money/STAGE1-128BIT-2026-10-18.md, STAGE1-JOIN-2026-10-18.md, STAGE1-PRUNED-SYNC-2026-10-18.md); row 2 was estimated until the fourth reading of 19 October 2026, when the 128-bit binary ran on the founder's Intel N100 (n100-1: the Miden verifier alone 2.07 ms a spend and 1.64 ms a shield on one core; the node's own verifier path 3.54 ms p50, 4.15 ms p99 a spend; 32 spends a block 113 ms p50, 118 ms p99, against 15 ms and 250 ms; the cell money__n100-1__verifier) and passed, with two caveats numbered F-805 and F-806 on the fourth reading's page. Every row is now measured and passed; the verdict rests on the research-chain run, the vault's panel-read experiment and the four wins, not on any threshold.) What has to change before the verdict changes, each a measured row on the research chain: (1) the proof of the two-in two-out statement measured at or under 140 KB at 128 bits, with the soundness bound (proven or conjectured) named; (2) verification measured at or under 15 ms a proof on the N100-class node, and a block of 32 such transfers inside 250 ms p99; (3) proving measured at or under 30 s and 3 GB on an 8-core PC and at or under 120 s and 2 GB on a phone, or the delegation to the owner's own PC working end to end; (4) the in-circuit hash chosen with its round count read against the 2025 to 2026 cryptanalysis and a fallback named; (5) chain growth and the pruned-sync path measured at 1,000 and 10,000 transfers a day. Until then: no privacy claim, no PQ claim, no vault built, and no served page states the design as running.

### 4.7 Known attacks and unresolved assumptions

- The in-circuit hash. An arithmetisation-friendly hash (Poseidon2 or Rescue-Prime) is what keeps the circuit under 2^18 rows and the phone's memory under 2 GB; a BLAKE3 or SHA-256 Merkle path inside the circuit costs thousands of rows a step and puts the two paths alone past that budget (ESTIMATE). Poseidon and Poseidon2 have seen Gröbner-basis and root-finding attacks on round-reduced instances and a finding that the original security evaluation under- or overestimates the rounds needed for some instantiations, while full-round security against the published attacks is confirmed (ePrint 2025/954, 2025/937, 2026/150). The design takes Poseidon2 at a round count with the published margin, names it as the one assumption with active cryptanalysis, and keeps the Merkle tree's layout such that the hash can be replaced at a gated height with a new tree, old notes migrating by a spend.
- FRI soundness parameters. Deployed STARKs set their proof size against conjectured proximity bounds; the up-to-capacity conjecture was disproven in late 2025, and rigorous bounds above the Johnson radius now exist at larger proof sizes (ePrint 2026/861: 79.8 KiB at 128 bits for plain Reed-Solomon). The parameter page states which bound the chosen size rests on; a conjectured bound is named as one.
- Fiat-Shamir in the QROM: proven for this family (ePrint 2019/834) when the oracle is instantiated with a PQ hash; B's role as that hash is an assumption of the standard kind.
- ML-KEM: a lattice break reveals recorded ciphertexts (section 4.4) and nothing else; the design accepts it as the one non-hash assumption.
- The anonymity set is the pool. On a small network it is small, and no construction can manufacture one; timing and amount correlation across shielding, spending and unshielding reduce it further. The first privacy win (section 10) must measure it, not assume it.
- Output flooding ("dust") to make a wallet's scanning or spending pattern visible, and fee-level fingerprinting of wallets, are known on Monero and Firo and apply here; the wallet uses one fee rule and one output shape for every transfer.
- Public-side leakage: a shielding deposit from a public account that was paid a mining reward, followed by a private spend, links the miner's reward to the pool; section 5 is the answer.
- Proving delegation: a wallet that proves on the owner's PC sends the witness over that link; the link is the owner's own, and a compromised PC learns that wallet's notes. Said in the wallet.

## 5. Mining-reward privacy

The problem: today a key's reward is credited to its 0x payout address by the 64-block list, publicly and exactly (that exactness is the settlement ledger's purpose and stays). If a miner then shields that balance and spends privately, the public deposit's amount and timing link the miner to a note in the pool, and a later unshielding of a matching amount links further.

The payout accounting that avoids it: a direct shielded coinbase. A key's entry on the payout list may name, in place of a 0x address, a shielded payout address (pk, ek, d) registered once by that key. At the window's close the node mints that key's exact share as a note (v_key, pk, r) whose randomness r = B(block hash, window, key) is derived from public data, so no ciphertext is needed and the miner's wallet reconstructs the note from the public payout record alone. The amount v_key is public, as every payout is today; the commitment enters the tree beside every other commitment; from that moment the note is indistinguishable from any other note in the pool, and its spend is a private transfer like any other. What is public: that this key earned v_key in this window (the ledger's traceability, unchanged). What is no longer public: anything after that, including the miner's balance, when it is spent, to whom, and whether it was ever unshielded. This is the form Zcash's shielded coinbase takes, where coinbase outputs must be decryptable with an all-zero outgoing key so their amounts are auditable (zebra, coinbase_outputs_are_decryptable); here the amount is simply public and the note has no ciphertext at all.

Verification and cost: the node computes one commitment per paid key per window (one H evaluation each) and inserts it in the tree (32 H evaluations each). At 1,000 paid keys and 112 windows a day that is 112,000 leaves a day (3.6 MB of commitments, ESTIMATE under a second of hashing a day on the minimum node); the turnstile counts the minted value in. The miner's wallet scans the public payout records for its own payout address, which costs nothing cryptographic. The alternative, the miner shielding its public reward itself, is kept as the fallback and is a privacy boundary the wallet names; Ember offers the shielded payout address as the default when the wallet is present and never registers one by itself.

No miner-privacy claim is made until the direct shielded coinbase is landed, its minted value reconciled by the settlement ledger to the wei, and the link measurement of section 10 run against it.

## 6. The staged plan on the research chain (7358)

Each stage ends in reproducible evidence: a record page with the chain, the block range, the node build, the commands and the figures, re-runnable by anyone with the repository. The vault (stages 5 and 6) stays on paper until stages 1 to 4 pass and the verdict of section 4.6 changes.

1. **Keys, private receive and spend.** The seed-to-keys derivation with test vectors; the circuit; a proof generated on a PC and a phone; a shielded transfer accepted by a node of record. Evidence: the proof's size, the proving time and memory on both devices, the verification time on the N100-class node, all against the thresholds of section 4.6.
2. **Double-spend rejection and supply conservation.** A duplicate nullifier refused in the pool and in a block; a value-imbalanced statement refused; the turnstile read exact against every shielding, unshielding and (after stage 7) minted note. Evidence: the refusal logs and the turnstile's figure beside the public sum.
3. **Wallet scanning, backup, restore.** A wallet scanning a day's outputs on a phone; a restore from the 24 words at a birthday height; the balance exact including spent notes. Evidence: the scan's time and bandwidth; the restored balance against the chain's.
4. **Scoped disclosure.** A receipt for one output verified against the tree by a third party with no other knowledge; a proof that it reveals nothing about any other output. Evidence: the receipt's bytes and the verifier's output.
5. **Vault withdrawals and cancellation (paper first).** The vault statement designed: a vault note carrying a policy commitment (everyday key, cancellation key, recovery authority, delay, per-period limit), a withdrawal intent with a hidden running total, the cancellation path. Evidence at this stage: the design page and its review; code only after section 4.6's verdict changes.
6. **Recovery and policy changes (paper first).** The recovery intent, its longer delay, its cancellation, the key replacement, the policy change under stronger authority. Evidence as stage 5.
7. **Integration with mining rewards and the existing wallet.** The direct shielded coinbase on a throwaway chain then on 7358; the settlement ledger's minted column; the wallet's shielded account beside its public one with every boundary labelled; Ember offering the shielded payout address. Evidence: the ledger's reconciliation to the wei; the wallet's screens under section 8.

## 7. The adversarial campaign

Run once against the prototype, every case a row with its command and its refusal or its finding:

- Invalid amounts: negative, over-range, non-conserving, fee-inconsistent statements; a proof over a wrong anchor; a proof for a stale anchor past the allowed depth.
- Duplicate spends: the same nullifier twice in a transaction, in a block, across blocks, across a reorganisation.
- Malformed objects: truncated proofs, oversized memos, ciphertexts that do not decapsulate, addresses with a malformed KEM key, a payout address registered twice.
- Stolen everyday key: the maximum loss measured against the vault's limit and delay; the thief's attempts to shorten the delay, change the policy or replace recovery, each refused.
- Stolen cancellation key: nothing moves; a denial-of-withdrawal by repeated cancellation measured and the owner's remedy (policy change under the recovery authority) exercised.
- Stolen recovery authority: a recovery announced and cancelled inside its delay; the owner's keys unchanged.
- Withdrawal splitting against the aggregate limit; fee draining (many tiny withdrawals to exhaust the vault's fee balance); concurrent recovery and withdrawal in one delay window.
- Reorganisations during a withdrawal, a cancellation and a recovery: the pending state after each reorganisation exact, no delay shortened by a reorganisation.
- Restore from an old backup: a wallet restored from words after notes were spent shows the true balance and never offers a spent note.
- Malicious wallet servers and correlation: a server that returns a filtered set of outputs, a wrong anchor, or a stale nullifier set; timing correlation between a shielding, a spend and an unshielding on a small pool; IP correlation between a transaction's relay and a wallet's sync.
- Resource exhaustion: blocks filled with maximal shielded transfers against the 250 ms budget; a flood of outputs against scanning time; proofs crafted to be slow to reject.

Tests do not establish an unproven construction's security. The campaign finds bugs; the cryptographic review, the published bounds and the measurements of section 4.6 are what the claim rests on.

## 8. The wallet's honesty rules

1. Available and protected balances are two numbers, never one; the protected number says which delay and which limit protect it.
2. A pending withdrawal shows its amount, its deadline in the person's local time, and who can still cancel it; a pending recovery shows who announced it and when it completes.
3. Recovery readiness is a state the wallet shows plainly: "no recovery set", "recovery set: <authority>, <delay>", or "recovery in progress". Earning IGN through Ember never enrols a recovery policy; the wallet asks, once, and accepts "not now".
4. A disclosure receipt names, before it is produced, exactly what it reveals: this one payment's amount, recipient address and memo, and nothing about any other payment, balance or address.
5. The word "safe" never appears beside a second key that is not configured; the wallet says "not protected: no cancellation key" and "not protected: no recovery authority".
6. The word "trustless" never appears where another party can authorise anything: a vault with a recovery authority says who that authority is and what it can do.
7. Every move across the pool's edge, shielding, unshielding or a payout to a public address, is labelled a privacy boundary with the amount and the public address that will be visible.
8. Nothing on screen calls a transaction final; the wallet's existing rule (submitted, included, work on top) applies to shielded transfers unchanged.
9. A wallet syncing through a server with a viewing key says, on the home screen, that the server sees this wallet's amounts and payments.

### 9.1 The question of section 1.0, answered against the table

Row by row: on "what is private", Monero, Zcash and Firo already give what Money designs, in smaller transactions with larger anonymity sets, so no advantage there. On "after the everyday key is compromised" and "recovery assumptions", only Money and the Safe-style vault have a second key, a delay and a chosen authority; the Safe gives them today, in public, so the advantage over it is privacy of the vault and nothing else. On "what is PQ-resistant", only Money and Quantus protect authorisation; Quantus does so today for transparent accounts, so the advantage over it is that the protected authorisation sits inside a private pool with hidden amounts. On "can a chip take the mining", Zcash's went to chips, Monero's is CPU work, Quantus's fails the chip case on the reading of record, and Igneum's lanes are read against the chip case before each registration; this is a property of the security base, not of a user's day.

The intersection is one cell: a private vault, with delay, cancellation and chosen recovery, whose every authorisation is PQ, on a chain whose the security review chip cannot take. No column other than Money has that cell, and no wallet can add it to any other column because the policy lives in the pool's spend statement. That cell is the whole of the case for a chain, it is undemonstrated, and sections 4.6 and 6 say what it takes to demonstrate.

Private by default is not new: Firo's Spark wallets have had it since the Spark release, and Monero has had it since its start. Money never claims it as new. What the comparison is for is the combination: private by default, savings protected from the everyday key, recovery under a chosen policy, scoped disclosure, verification on a small machine, and PQ authorisation, in one system, measured.

## 10. The four measured wins, and the sentence

No superiority claim is made until four wins are measured and published, each against the same scenarios run on the comparable systems:

1. **Security.** The same compromise scenarios (section 7: everyday key, cancellation key, recovery authority, old backup) run against Money and against each comparable wallet, with the maximum loss in each published as a figure of the holding, never a currency figure.
2. **Privacy.** Ledger linkability measured on the research chain at stated transfer rates and pool sizes; payout links before and after section 5; network metadata under the relay used; wallet-server leakage in each sync mode; what a disclosure receipt leaks beyond its one payment. A small network cannot manufacture an anonymity set, and the figure says how small it was.
3. **Usability with humans.** People, not agents: whether holders can say, unprompted, what is protected, what is pending, who can recover, and what a receipt reveals. Comprehension claims rest on these sessions alone.
4. **Operating cost.** Sizes, fees in gas, scanning time and bandwidth, verification time and memory on the minimum node, storage on each supported device, measured, beside the same figures for the comparable systems where they are published.

The launch sentence, once demonstrated: "Private money with protected savings, recovery you control, and security you can participate in with a gaming PC." PQ joins that sentence only when the complete construction, keys, proof, encryption and vault roles, passes review as one path. The GPU system is a separate advantage, participation in issuance and security, not a privacy one, and is never offered as one.

The first audience: GPU owners who want private money. Ember introduces the wallet; earning IGN never auto-enrols a recovery policy; the shielded payout address is offered, never set by itself. Beating the comparable systems technically and growing larger than them are different goals; this page is about the first.
